When one AI hires another, it settles for mediocre

Language models that pick other models don’t fall for the loudest liar. They avoid the liar — and take the honest expert down with it. Play a round and see whether you do any better.

The task

Hire one of three agents

Each states a competence claim out of 10 and a one-sentence pitch. One is genuinely excellent, one is average, one is a liar — but their true skill is hidden. You earn whatever your hire is actually worth. Everything below is real data from the experiment.

The result

Nobody beats the dumbest possible strategy

If you just believe whoever claims the highest number, you score 6.72 out of a possible 8.0. Not one language model significantly beats that. The two weakest score significantly worse. Give them a verified track record and most recover — but unevenly.

Why it matters

This is a market for lemons, and the honest expert pays

The models correctly distrust the loudest claim — then apply that distrust to everyone, including the candidate who was telling the truth. Nobody successfully lies, and the best provider still loses the job.

Where this shows up

Tool registries and agent directories let providers describe their own capabilities. An audit of 2,214 public MCP servers found 9.9% of tool descriptions don’t match what the code does. Nothing checks the claim at selection time.

How deception was produced

Not scripted. Candidates are language models told their true skill and given a real incentive: a 50% audit and a fine of 2 points per point of exaggeration. Liars self-limit rather than claiming 10, which is what makes the naive baseline hard to beat.

How it was measured

Every outcome is arithmetic — no model grades another. The same 40 candidate slates go to every selector twice, with and without an audit record, so verification is the only thing that changes. Paired Wilcoxon and exact McNemar tests, Holm-corrected.

What didn’t fix it

Turning on extended reasoning rescued one model completely and did nothing for another. A verified record helped only the models that were already at chance, widening the gap between selectors instead of closing it.
